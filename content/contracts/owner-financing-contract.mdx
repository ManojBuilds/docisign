Creative real estate financing made simple. This contract protects both the buyer and seller with clear terms on monthly payments, interest, and property taxes.

## What's inside this financing contract?

A comprehensive promissory note and security agreement specifically for real estate.

### Key Features

- **Interest Rate & Amortization**: Clearly defines how interest is calculated and how much of each payment goes toward the principal.
- **Balloon Payment Clause**: A critical section for sellers who don't want to wait 30 years for their money, requiring the buyer to pay the balance in a shorter time frame.
- **Tax & Insurance Escrow**: Specifies who is responsible for paying property taxes and maintaining homeowners insurance to protect the asset.
- **Late Fee & Default Terms**: Provides the seller with clear legal recourse if the buyer falls behind on payments.

## Why skip the bank?

Owner financing allows for faster closings, lower closing costs, and the ability to sell properties that might not qualify for traditional bank loans.

- **Flexibility**: Negotiate terms that work for both parties.
- **Speed**: Close in days instead of months.
- **Passive Income**: Sellers earn interest instead of just a lump sum.

## Common Questions

### What is Owner Financing?

Owner financing (or seller financing) is a real estate transaction where the seller acts as the lender. Instead of the buyer getting a mortgage from a bank, they make monthly payments directly to the seller.

### What interest rate should I charge for owner financing?

Typically, owner financing interest rates are higher than bank rates to compensate the seller for the risk. A common range is 5% to 10%, but this is entirely negotiable between the parties.

### What is a Balloon Payment?

Many owner financing deals have a 'balloon payment' due after a few years (e.g., 5 years). This means the buyer makes small monthly payments for a while, then pays off the entire remaining balance in one large sum, usually by refinancing with a bank.

### What happens if the buyer defaults?

The contract includes a 'Default and Remedies' clause. If the buyer stops paying, the seller can begin the process of taking the property back (foreclosure) and usually keeps all payments made to that point as liquidated damages.
